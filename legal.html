Two prohibitions, one definition gap

The law against manual scavenging is not weak because it is gentle. On paper it is stern: its offences are non-bailable, companies answer through their officers, and the Supreme Court has re-issued the same orders for a decade. It goes quiet in three places — in what it defines, in who must complain, and in who the state agrees to count.

Exhibit 01 showed that the labour was assigned by birth. This exhibit asks what the state did with that fact once it decided to act. The answer is a statute that bans an inheritance by regulating an employment relationship, and then draws its central definition so that most of the people who are dying fall just outside the headline category.

What 2013 changed, and what it did not

The 1993 Act made it an offence to employ a manual scavenger or build a dry latrine. Critics note it targeted employers while leaving the demand — latrines with no water supply — untouched, which pushed the practice out of the record rather than out of existence.3 The 2013 Act is a visibly different instrument. It prohibits unsanitary latrines outright, outlaws all manual cleaning of excreta from them and from the drains and pits they empty into, brings sewers and septic tanks inside the law for the first time, adds a duty to rehabilitate, and says plainly in its preamble that it exists to correct a historical injustice.1,4

It also contains a provision that is easy to skim past. Section 33 makes it the duty of every local authority and agency to use appropriate technological appliances for cleaning sewers and septic tanks, with a view to ending manual handling, and makes it the government's duty to promote that technology through funding and incentives.1 That is the legal hook for Exhibit 05: since 2013, mechanisation has not been a policy preference. It has been a statutory obligation.

Object 01 — The statute: how the Act defines who counts

PRIMARY SOURCE / EXHIBIT 01

ACT NO. 25 OF 2013

The Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act, 2013

A statute that prohibits manual scavenging, prohibits hazardous cleaning of sewers and septic tanks, and establishes rehabilitation obligations.

→ Read the official text of the Act

Two definitions in Section 2 decide almost everything. They are summarised here in plain language; the exact wording is in the official text linked at the foot of the page.1

Manual scavenger

Act 25 of 2013, s.2(1)(g)
WHO
A person engaged or employed, on a regular or contract basis, by an individual, a local authority, an agency or a contractor.
DOING WHAT
Manually cleaning, carrying, disposing of or otherwise handling human excreta.
WHERE
An unsanitary latrine; an open drain or pit that such a latrine empties into; a railway track; or other places the government notifies.
WHEN
Before the excreta has fully decomposed.
EXCEPTION
Someone doing the work with devices and protective gear notified by the Centre is not a manual scavenger.
A municipal sewer line is not on this list. A sewer worker is therefore not a "manual scavenger" in the narrow, definitional sense.

Hazardous cleaning

Act 25 of 2013, s.2(1)(d) and s.7
WHO
An employee.
DOING WHAT
Manually cleaning a sewer or septic tank.
WHAT MAKES IT "HAZARDOUS"
The employer has not provided protective gear and cleaning devices, or has not ensured the prescribed safety precautions.
THE PROHIBITION
Section 7 bars any person, local authority or agency from employing anyone for it, "directly or indirectly".
The offence is defined by what the employer failed to supply, not by the act of entering. And it is a separate category, so deaths here are reported as "hazardous cleaning" or sewer deaths.

The 2013 Rules add a second layer. They list the gear an employer must supply for sewer and septic-tank work — gas monitors, emergency oxygen-resuscitator kits, safety belts, goggles, helmets and more — and state that no one is to clean a sewer manually, even with that gear, except in the limited circumstances the Rules specify.5 So the Act's definition ties the offence to missing safeguards, while the Rules go further and ask for an end to routine manual entry. Read together, they describe a system that wants manual sewer entry to be exceptional. Read separately, they let the headline category stay empty while the deaths continue.

What the statute threatens

The penalties are set in two tiers, and they are modest on their face. The tier for hazardous sewer cleaning is the heavier one.1

OffenceSectionFirst convictionRepeat
Building an unsanitary latrine; engaging a manual scavenger, directly or indirectly; breaching the voiding of old contracts5, 6 → 8Up to 1 year, or ₹50,000, or bothUp to 2 years, or ₹1 lakh, or both
Engaging anyone for hazardous cleaning of a sewer or septic tank, directly or indirectly7 → 9Up to 2 years, or ₹2 lakh, or bothUp to 5 years, or ₹5 lakh, or both

Around those penalties sit five procedural rules that decide whether they are ever used.

  • Cognizable and non-bailable (s.22): police can arrest without a warrant, and bail is not a right.
  • Company liability (s.23): the company and the people running it are deemed guilty, and directors, managers or officers can be proceeded against where the offence is attributable to their consent, connivance or neglect.
  • Executive Magistrate trial (s.21): states may confer first-class magistrate powers on executive magistrates, and offences may be tried summarily.
  • A three-month window (s.10): no court takes cognizance except on a complaint made within three months of the offence.
  • An exemption valve (s.39): the government may exempt an area, class of buildings or class of persons from provisions of the Act for up to six months at a time, with reasons recorded.
READING THE TABLE

The law is written for the person who hires, not for the person who is lowered. That is correct in principle — the worker is protected, not punished — but it makes the statute depend entirely on someone being prepared to name an employer. In a contracting chain, naming one is exactly the hard part. That is Exhibit 03.

Enforcement: where the stern law goes quiet

1. A count that stops at the definition

In a written reply in the Lok Sabha, the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment gave year-wise figures for deaths during sewer and septic-tank cleaning between 1 January 2019 and 30 June 2026, totalling 498, and added that no death had been reported due to manual scavenging.2

Independent counts are higher. For 2025 the Safai Karamchari Andolan reports 121 deaths, against the ministry's 47, and puts the number since 2013 above 1,700.8 A second advocacy group, the Dalit Adivasi Shakti Adhikar Manch, cites 116 for 2024 and 158 for 2025.9 The three counters are not measuring identical things, but a gap of two to three times for the same year is the legal layer's problem in miniature: whatever the law cannot classify, the state does not tally.

2. Compensation as the substitute for prosecution

Where a death is counted, the usual state response is a payment. The Supreme Court fixed ₹10 lakh per sewer death in 2014 and raised it to ₹30 lakh in October 2023.6,10 The Lok Sabha reply sorted the 498 deaths by what had been recorded as paid:2

Full ₹10 lakh category345
Full ₹30 lakh category75
Partial payment22
Payment not confirmed56

In 78 of 498 cases the data shows partial payment or does not confirm payment. For 2025 alone, payment could not be confirmed in 20 of the 47 recorded deaths.2

Compensation is the right thing to pay and a poor thing to rely on. It arrives after the death, it does not require anyone to be found responsible, and it can sit comfortably beside a prosecution rate of close to nothing. Parliamentary information summarised in 2022 noted that, from the Act's commencement to mid-2019, no state or Union territory had filed a conviction report under it, and a Karnataka commission's data showed 36 incidents since 2008 with 72 deaths, police cases in every one, and no conviction.11 An earlier analysis found that national crime statistics had recorded no offence at all under the 1993 Act.12 In Vasai-Virar, eight accused in the 2019 septic-tank deaths were briefly arrested and released on bail, and the case has not moved since; in the 2020 case the accused were not arrested.7 Registration under the special law does happen: after the death of Panth Lal Chandra in Delhi's New Friends Colony in March 2025, police booked a case under s.105 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita and ss.7 and 9 of the Act, even as the Delhi Jal Board denied that the men were its staff.23 The open question is what happens after the FIR.

3. A procedural trap in the complaint route

Section 10 says courts may take cognizance only on a complaint within three months. In December 2023 a single judge of the Karnataka High Court quashed proceedings against a man accused of personally lowering a labourer into a manhole without safety equipment, on the ground that the complaint should have gone to the magistrate rather than the police. A critical analysis argues that this misreads s.10 against s.22, which makes offences cognizable, and notes that victims' families usually have only the police station as an accessible door.13 Whether or not that critique is right, it shows how a short, unqualified sentence can decide whether an FIR lives.

OBJECT 02 — PHOTOGRAPHS BEFORE THE COURT

Manual drain cleaning at Gate F of the Supreme Court

Photographs annexed to applications by amicus curiae K. Parameshwar in Dr. Balram Singh v. Union of India; order of 6 August 2025. The images are part of the court record and are not reproduced here.

On 6 August 2025 the bench of Justices Sudhanshu Dhulia and Aravind Kumar recorded that it had been shown photographs of manual scavenging and hazardous cleaning at Gate F of the Court itself, and directed the Public Works Department to reply. On 18 September 2025 a bench of Justices Aravind Kumar and N. V. Anjaria fined the Delhi PWD ₹5 lakh, observing that the Court's October 2023 directions seemed to have been consciously ignored. The labourers, reported to include a minor, had been engaged through contractors without protective gear. The bench warned that any future incident would lead to FIRs against officials, and the amicus said a complaint had been sought to be filed at Tilak Marg police station without action by police or PWD.14,15

WHY THIS OBJECT

Most evidence in this exhibit is paper — statutes, replies, tables. This is the rare case where the paper and the street meet at the court's own gate, and the court is the witness. It also shows the enforcement chain in one frame: a public authority, a contractor, workers without gear, and a complaint that goes nowhere until a bench intervenes. The amicus asked that the fine be recovered from the officers themselves, which would be a very different kind of consequence from compensation paid to families.

The courts keep re-issuing the same order

Read in sequence, the judicial record is a series of corrections to a state that keeps reporting success.

  1. 2014
    Safai Karamchari Andolan v. Union of India. On 27 March the Court orders ₹10 lakh for each sewer death since 1993 and rehabilitation of manual scavengers.10
  2. Oct 2023
    Dr. Balram Singh v. Union of India. Fourteen directions: complete eradication in phases, compensation raised to ₹30 lakh for a sewer death (₹20 lakh where the victim is disabled), a national survey, and functioning monitoring bodies.6,16
  3. Dec 2024
    The bench finds the Union's status report unsatisfactory, records a suggestion from the Bar that contempt proceedings be considered for wilful disobedience, and orders a fuller affidavit with state-wise breakups.17
  4. Jan 2025
    On 29 January the Court orders manual scavenging and hazardous cleaning stopped in Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata, Bengaluru and Hyderabad.18 It records the Union's claim that 456 of 775 districts had no manual scavenging or manual sewer cleaning, and notes a lack of clarity about the metros.19
  5. Aug–Sep 2025
    Gate F, as above: a public authority is fined ₹5 lakh and warned of FIRs.14,15
  6. Jan 2026
    On 20 January the Court corrects the Union and fills a vacancy it had long flagged. See below.20

The order of 20 January 2026

This one daily order is worth reading closely, because three of its paragraphs map directly onto the argument of this exhibit.20

  • The "nil" claim is narrower than it sounded. The Union's affidavit said a fresh survey had been conducted in 465 of 775 districts and found no manual scavengers, and that by 22 November 2025 a total of 643 districts had uploaded nil certificates. The Court clarified that the affidavit says nothing about a survey of manual sewer cleaning, and modified its earlier order by deleting the words "and manual sewer cleaning". The definition gap, in a Supreme Court order.
  • The compensation rule was clarified, not softened. Cases where ₹10 lakh had already been paid or determined by 20 October 2023 are not reopened. Where compensation for an earlier death had not been paid or determined by then, states and municipal authorities must pay ₹30 lakh. The clarification was sought because different High Courts had read the 2023 direction differently.
  • The monitor had no head. The National Commission for Safai Karamcharis, which Section 31 charges with monitoring the Act, was without a Chairperson, Vice-Chairperson and a Member, with four member posts vacant. The Union had assured the Court on 19 November 2025 that appointments would be expedited, and by 20 January had made no further progress. The Court declined a request for four more weeks.

The rehabilitation promise, in law and in arithmetic

Section 13 of the Act is unusually specific. Within a month of being listed, an identified manual scavenger is to receive a photo identity card and one-time cash assistance, children are entitled to scholarships, and the person is to be offered a residential plot or house with financial help, livelihood training with a stipend of at least ₹3,000 a month, and subsidy and concessional loan for an alternative occupation. The District Magistrate is made responsible for each person's rehabilitation.1

Against that, the Centre reported that 58,098 manual scavengers had been identified in the 2013 and 2018 surveys and each given ₹40,000 in cash; 22,294 had received skill training and 2,313 a capital subsidy.21 Section 13 lists six entitlements; the reported delivery concentrates on the first and cheapest. The identification itself is contested: the Supreme Court's amicus noted that identified numbers moved implausibly between surveys, such as from zero to 7,378 in Maharashtra between 2013 and 2018.22

WHAT THIS LAYER ESTABLISHES

The law's weakness is not mainly its text. It is the interface between the text and three kinds of actor. The classifier decides which deaths are "manual scavenging". The complainant must act inside a short window against an employer who may be several contractors away. And the monitor must be staffed. When all three fail together, a non-bailable offence can be committed in public, as at Gate F, with a very low chance of consequence.
