A Job That Legally Does Not Exist

India has banned manual scavenging twice. Workers are still dying inside sewers roughly every three days.

This exhibit treats manual scavenging not as a leftover of the past but as an outcome the present system keeps producing — through caste history, statutory definitions, municipal plumbing, contracting chains, unused technology, and responsibility that dissolves the moment someone dies.

THE PROBLEM IN ONE SCREEN

Two countries, one set of sewers

Everything in this exhibit follows from a single contradiction. The state's own records say manual scavenging has been eliminated. The state's own records also say people keep dying inside drains. Both statements are produced by the same ministry, in the same year, sometimes in the same document.

WHAT THE STATE COUNTS
  • 58,098 manual scavengers identified in the only two national surveys ever run, in 2013 and 2018; all were paid ₹40,000 in one-time cash assistance4
  • 714 of 766 districts had declared themselves manual-scavenging-free as of November 20235
  • A fresh survey under the 2013 Act found no manual scavengers in any district6
  • 90,942 sewer and septic-tank workers profiled under the NAMASTE scheme, 87,037 issued PPE kits7
WHAT OTHER COUNTS SHOW
  • 7,40,078 households where human excreta was removed by another human being — Census 20112
  • 1,82,505 manual scavengers in rural India alone — Socio Economic and Caste Census 20118
  • 42,303 people still working as manual scavengers across 18 states as of August 2019 — National Commission for Safai Karamcharis9
  • 1,726+ sewer and septic-tank deaths recorded since 2013 by the Safai Karamchari Andolan, which puts the rate at about one death every 45 hours10
WHY BOTH COLUMNS CAN BE TRUE AT ONCE

The 2013 Act defines a manual scavenger by where the excreta is: an unsanitary latrine, an open drain, a pit, a railway track. Entering a sewer line or a septic tank is regulated separately, as hazardous cleaning. So a worker who suffocates inside a municipal sewer has not, in the statutory sense, died of manual scavenging. The denial is not necessarily a lie. It is a definition doing work. Exhibit 02 takes this apart clause by clause.

SEVENTY-SEVEN YEARS OF TRYING

The repeal that keeps needing repeating

Read as a sequence, India's response to manual scavenging is not a story of neglect. It is a story of unusually persistent intervention that has not changed the outcome — which is the harder thing to explain.

  1. 1949
    Barve Committee, Bombay. The first official inquiry into how scavengers actually live, and into fixing their minimum wages. It treats the work as a job to be improved, not an assignment to be ended.11
  2. 1955
    Protection of Civil Rights Act. Compelling any person to practise scavenging on the ground of untouchability becomes an offence, eight years after Article 17 abolished untouchability on paper.12
  3. 1957–68
    Malkani and Pandya Committees. Malkani's report identifies the customary claims that bound families to particular households — recorded under local names such as gharaki, jajmani, virat and dastoori — and recommends underground drainage and improved latrines as the technical exit.13
  4. 1989
    Low Cost Sanitation for Liberation of Scavengers (ILCS). A national scheme to convert dry latrines into pour-flush pit latrines. The same year, the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act makes forcing a Scheduled Caste person to handle human excreta a specific atrocity.14
  5. 1993
    First prohibition. The Employment of Manual Scavengers and Construction of Dry Latrines (Prohibition) Act criminalises employing a manual scavenger. It comes into force only on 26 January 1997, and states adopt it at different times. Critics note it targeted employers while leaving the demand — dry latrines with no water supply — untouched, pushing the practice out of the record rather than out of existence.15
  6. 2013
    Second prohibition. The Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act, passed on 6 September, assented to on 18 September and in force from 6 December, widens the ban to all manual handling of excreta, prohibits unsanitary latrines outright, regulates sewer and septic-tank entry, and adds a statutory duty to rehabilitate — cash assistance, scholarships, skill training, housing.16
  7. 2014
    Safai Karamchari Andolan v. Union of India. The Supreme Court orders that families of everyone who died in sewage work since 1993 be identified and paid ₹10 lakh each. Swachh Bharat Mission launches the same year, directing most of its money at building new toilets rather than converting unsanitary ones.17
  8. 2023
    Dr. Balram Singh v. Union of India. On 20 October the Court issues fourteen directions, raises sewer-death compensation to ₹30 lakh and disability compensation to ₹20 lakh, and orders a phased end to manual sewer entry. The NAMASTE scheme launches to mechanise cleaning and equip workers.3
  9. 2025
    An order specifically for six cities. Finding compliance unsatisfactory, the Court on 29 January directs a complete stop to manual scavenging and hazardous cleaning in Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata, Bengaluru and Hyderabad — an admission that a nationwide ban had not reached the largest municipal systems.18
  10. 2026
    The count continues. Parliament is told 498 workers died in sewers and septic tanks between January 2019 and June 2026, that compensation could not be confirmed in 56 of those cases, and that no death has been reported due to manual scavenging.1

A NOTE ON METHOD

How this exhibit is built

Each layer is anchored by objects rather than by argument alone: a photograph, a statute, a film, a memoir, a government reply. Objects are described and analysed here, and linked to their sources; copyrighted photographs and film stills are not reproduced. Where the state's numbers and independent counts disagree, both are shown rather than reconciled, because the disagreement is itself the finding.
